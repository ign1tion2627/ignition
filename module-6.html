Final Design Challenge

Everything you've learned, applied to one real engineering problem. No equipment needed — just your brain and a pencil.

The Challenge

A small town has a road with a sharp, sudden bump right before a stop sign — cars that hit it too fast get jolted hard, and a few have even lost control briefly. The town doesn't have the budget to repave the whole road right now. They've asked you, an engineer, to propose a solution.

Your job

Design a solution using what you've learned about suspension, braking, shape, or road design. There's no single right answer — the goal is to think like an engineer: identify the problem clearly, propose a solution, and explain why it would work.

How to Approach It

  1. Define the real problem. Is it the bump itself, how fast cars are going over it, how suspension responds to it, or something else? Engineers often find that the real problem is more specific than it first sounds.
  2. Brainstorm at least 3 different solutions. They can be about the road, the cars, warning signs, speed control — anything. Don't judge them yet, just get ideas down.
  3. Pick your best idea and sketch it. A simple drawing is enough — labels matter more than artistic skill.
  4. Explain your reasoning. Why does this solution work? What engineering concept from this course does it rely on?
  5. Consider a trade-off. Every engineering solution costs something — money, time, comfort, or convenience. What's the trade-off of your solution, and is it worth it?
✏️ The main activity — write this out

On paper, write out your full response to the 5 steps above. Aim for at least one full paragraph explaining your chosen solution and one sentence naming its trade-off. There's no need to submit this anywhere — it's yours to keep, and it's the kind of thinking real engineers do every day.

A Few Directions to Consider

If you're stuck, here are a few angles other students have used to think through problems like this one — not answers, just starting points: something about how a car's suspension responds to sudden bumps, something about driver visibility and warning signs before the bump, something about the actual shape or slope of the bump itself, or something about speed near the stop sign.

You finished the course

You've now gone through how engines work, how electricity flows through a car, how gas and electric vehicles compare, how shape affects performance, and how brakes and safety systems protect people — and applied all of it to a real design problem. That's the same core thinking process real automotive engineers use every day.
