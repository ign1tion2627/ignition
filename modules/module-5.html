Brakes, Suspension & Safety

How a two-ton car stops on command, and why "crumpling" in a crash is actually the point.

How Brakes Actually Stop a Car

Brakes don't magically stop a car — they convert the car's motion into heat through friction. When you press the brake pedal, brake pads squeeze tightly against a spinning metal disc attached to each wheel. That squeezing friction slows the wheel's spin, and the tires' grip on the road does the rest, slowing the whole car.

Where does the energy go?

All the energy of a moving car has to go somewhere when it stops. Brakes convert it into heat — which is why brake discs can get hot enough to glow after hard braking on a mountain road.

Suspension: Absorbing the Bumps

Every road has small bumps, dips, and cracks. Without suspension, every one of those bumps would jolt straight into the passengers and could even bounce the tires off the ground momentarily, losing grip. A car's suspension — springs and shock absorbers at each wheel — lets each wheel move up and down independently, soaking up bumps while keeping the car's body level and the tires pressed to the road.

The springs compress and extend independently at each wheel, absorbing bumps in the road.

Crumple Zones: Designed to Break

This one surprises most people: parts of a car are intentionally designed to crumple and deform in a crash. That's not a flaw — it's a safety feature. A crash involves a huge amount of energy that has to go somewhere. If the car's frame were perfectly rigid, all of that energy would transfer directly to the passengers. Instead, the front and rear sections are engineered to crumple in a controlled way, absorbing the crash energy before it reaches the passenger cabin — which is built to stay as rigid as possible.

Two zones, two jobs

Crumple zones (front and rear) are meant to deform and absorb energy. The passenger cabin is built as a rigid "safety cage" that resists crushing, protecting the people inside while the crumple zones do the absorbing around them.
